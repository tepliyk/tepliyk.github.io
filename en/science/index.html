Science from a balcony: variable stars for the AAVSO

1,159 measurements for the AAVSO, 59 objects, 21 nights since 20 August 2026

Besides pretty pictures, the telescope on my balcony also measures. Frames of nebulae and galaxies hold hundreds of stars, and some of them are variable: their brightness changes over hours or days. I measure that brightness and submit the results to the AAVSO, the American Association of Variable Star Observers.

For more than a century the AAVSO has collected observations from amateurs all over the world. Professional astronomers use this data in research: to refine periods of eclipsing binaries, catch outbursts, decide when to point a large telescope. My observer code is KTFA, and everything submitted under it is open.

Below is everything submitted so far: light curves, the list of stars and how exactly I measure with a small smart telescope under a light-polluted city sky.

Supernova SN 2026aaiv in NGC 7331

A type Ia supernova flared up in September 2026 in the galaxy NGC 7331, about 44 million light years away. I measured its brightness near the peak: 5 measurements from 12 September to 1 October 2026, between magnitude 11.94 and 12.56.

Highlights

A few results that best show what a 30 mm lens can do: a full pulsation cycle in one night, long runs of eclipsing binaries and the faint limit. Scroll sideways.

SZ Lyn

A HADS pulsator with a period of 2 h 54 min. One night, 4/5 November, covered a full cycle: 61 points every 3 min, from 9.69 to 9.15 (VSX gives 9.72 to 9.08). The check star held to 0.010 mag, so the shape is real, not noise. Maximum came 7.5 min after the old linear ephemeris: the period of SZ Lyn is known to change, and measurements like this track it.

V Tri

An eclipsing binary (EB), period 0.5852 d. 163 measurements over 6 nights from 25 August to 7 October: both the drop into eclipse and the quiet stretches between, almost a full magnitude of amplitude (10.73 to 11.68). The longest run on this page, taken as a by-product of imaging the galaxy M 33.

163 measurements · 6 nights · brightness 10.73…11.68 · 25 August – 7 October 2026

BC Tri

A contact binary (EW), period 0.7078 d, same M 33 field. 134 measurements over 4 nights, amplitude only 0.24 (11.43 to 11.67). A small amplitude at 11th magnitude: about the limit of what 30 mm shows with confidence.

134 measurements · 4 nights · brightness 11.43…11.67 · 25 August – 2 October 2026

V1188 Tau

A contact binary (EW), period 0.5759 d, in the Pleiades field. 87 measurements over two nights, 20 to 23 August, from 11.93 to 12.44: a good part of the cycle passes in one night and shows up in the curve.

87 measurements · 2 nights · brightness 11.93…12.44 · 20 August – 23 August 2026

YZ Tau

An RRc star, period 0.4115 d, from 13.02 to 13.44. The faintest object with a curve on this page: 13th magnitude with a 30 mm lens from a city balcony, 78 measurements over two nights.

78 measurements · 2 nights · brightness 13.02…13.44 · 20 August – 23 August 2026

Light curves

The higher the point, the brighter the star. Each segment of a card is one night: the gaps between nights are squeezed out so you can see how the brightness changed during the night. Shown are stars with 10 or more measurements, long series with up to 200 points.

How I measure

  • Telescope: DWARF Mini, 30 mm lens, 150 mm focal length, Sony IMX662 colour sensor.
  • Band: the green channel of the colour sensor. The AAVSO records it as TG; it is close to the standard V band.
  • Comparison stars: an ensemble of comparison stars from AAVSO VSP charts plus a separate check star.
  • Calibration: dark frames and flat fields. Frames are stacked in groups, and each point is timed at the middle of its group.
  • Precision: the median error of a single point is 0.03 mag; for fainter stars it is larger.
  • Limits: a city sky, a small aperture and a non-standard filter. Bright stars with amplitudes of a few tenths of a magnitude work best.

Open data

All measurements are in the international AAVSO database and available to anyone by observer code KTFA or by star name. Stacked images and processing guides are in the astro-data repository on GitHub.
